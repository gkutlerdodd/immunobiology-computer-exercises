Computer exercise X - Finding protective HLA alleles against SARS-CoV-2 infection

Augusto et al. (2023, Nature) found that people carrying the HLA allele HLA-B*15:01 were substantially more likely to remain asymptomatic following SARS-CoV-2 infection. They provided evidence that this protection was associated with the ability of HLA-B*15:01 to present both the SARS-CoV-2 spike epitope NQKLIANQF and highly similar epitopes from the spike proteins of two seasonal coronaviruses. As a result, people carrying HLA-B*15:01 who had previously been exposed to these seasonal coronaviruses may already have had memory T cells capable of recognizing SARS-CoV-2 during their first infection.

The authors first compared HLA frequencies between people with symptomatic and asymptomatic SARS-CoV-2 infections and found that HLA-B*15:01 was significantly overrepresented in the asymptomatic group. However, HLA-B*15:01 was already relatively common in the symptomatic cohort, with a frequency of nearly 5%, and its frequency exceeded 11% in the asymptomatic cohort. Many other HLA alleles occurred at substantially lower frequencies. This raises an important question: could other HLA alleles also present cross-reactive coronavirus epitopes but have been missed because of their low frequency in this cohort?

In this computer practical, you will investigate this question using bioinformatics tools. You will first learn how to use the Immune Epitope Database (IEDB) to identify coronavirus epitopes known to elicit a T-cell response. You will then use NetMHCpan to predict peptide-HLA binding and investigate whether other HLA alleles can present cross-reactive epitopes.

Finding experimentally validated epitopes in IEDB

The Immune Epitope Database (IEDB) is a database of experimentally characterized epitopes associated with T- or B-cell immune responses. Because one of the most challenging parts of studying the immune response to a pathogen is determining which parts of the pathogen are recognized by the immune system, IEDB is a valuable resource for both experimental and computational analyses.

Finding candidate protective HLA alleles

Now that you have identified potential pairs of cross-reactive epitopes, the next question is whether any HLA molecule can present both peptides in a pair. If an HLA molecule binds both a SARS-CoV-2 epitope and a related seasonal coronavirus epitope, it could potentially provide a mechanism for pre-existing T-cell immunity against SARS-CoV-2.

For this analysis, we will use NetMHCpan to perform in silico predictions of MHC-peptide binding. Open NetMHCpan and familiarize yourself with the available input options.

TipHint 2: HLA selection

There are many possible ways to select HLA alleles to test. One approach is to use the alleles found in the HLA supertype representative list. HLA supertypes are groups of alleles that present peptides with similar physiochemical properties. Using representative alleles from different supertypes therefore provides a relatively diverse set of HLA molecules to test.

TipHint 3: Analyzing the NetMHCpan predictions

For each candidate epitope pair, look for HLA alleles predicted to be strong binders of both peptides. These are the alleles that could potentially present both the SARS-CoV-2 and seasonal coronavirus epitopes.

Odds ratio is defined as \(\frac{f_{asymp}/(1-f_{asymp})}{f_{symp}/(1-f_{symp})}\), where \(f_{asymp}\) and \(f_{symp}\) are the frequencies of an allele in the asymptomatic and symptomatic cohorts, respectively. We exclude very rare alleles from this calculation because small differences in their frequencies can produce very large and potentially misleading odds ratios.

Surprisingly, HLA-B*15:01 is not the allele with the highest odds ratio! Based on the allele frequencies in the study population, why do you think HLA-B*15:01 was the only allele reported to be statistically significant?

Project ideas

If you decide to use this computer exercise for your poster presentation, here are some ideas to get you started:

  • Expand the epitope dataset. Include additional coronavirus species in your IEDB search, or investigate longer epitopes rather than restricting your analysis to peptides of 8-12 amino acids.
  • Investigate statistical power. Calculate statistical tests or confidence intervals for HLA allele enrichment in the asymptomatic cohort. Can you determine why HLA-B*15:01 reaches statistical significance while other alleles with similarly high odds ratios do not?
  • Analyze the alleles from the study cohort. Use the HLA alleles from the Augusto et al. study population as inputs to NetMHCpan. How well does your analysis align with the observed odds ratios?
  • Use structures for mechanistic explanations. If you are feeling ambitious, use available protein structures or structural prediction tools to investigate why particular HLA molecules might be able to present both members of a cross-reactive epitope pair.
  • Or, of course, your own creative idea!
